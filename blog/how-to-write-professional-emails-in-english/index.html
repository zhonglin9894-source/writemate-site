How to Write Professional Emails in English (Non-Native Speaker Guide)

By NativeProse Team · July 1, 2025 · 10 min read

Writing professional emails in English is one of the most important skills for non-native speakers in international workplaces. A well-written email builds credibility. A poorly written one — even if grammatically correct — can leave the wrong impression.

The challenge for ESL writers isn't usually grammar. You know the rules. The problem is that certain phrases, structures, and word choices that feel natural in your first language sound strange or overly formal in English. Native speakers notice these patterns even when they can't explain exactly why.

This guide covers everything: structure, tone, common mistakes, and ready-to-use templates.

The 5-Part Email Structure

Every professional email follows the same basic structure. Once you internalize this, the writing becomes much easier.

  1. Subject line — clear, specific, tells the reader what this is about
  2. Greeting — sets the tone (formal or casual)
  3. Opening line — context or reason for writing
  4. Body — your main message (one topic per email)
  5. Closing + sign-off — action item, thank you, or next step

The most common mistake ESL writers make is writing emails that are too long. In English professional culture, shorter is almost always better. Get to the point quickly. Native speakers appreciate it.

Writing Subject Lines That Get Opened

Your subject line determines whether the email gets read. Good subject lines are specific and direct — they tell the reader exactly what the email is about.

✗ Too Vague

"Question" / "Checking in" / "Hi" / "Follow up"

✓ Specific and Clear

"Question about the Q3 report deadline" / "Following up on our Tuesday meeting" / "Action needed: approval for budget request"

A useful formula: [Topic] + [Specific detail]. For example: "Project proposal — feedback needed by Friday" or "Rescheduling Thursday's call to next week."

Tip: If you need the recipient to do something, say so in the subject. "Action required" or "Response needed" signals urgency clearly without being rude.

Professional Openings That Sound Natural

How you open an email sets the tone for everything that follows. ESL writers often make this more complicated than it needs to be.

Greetings

SituationUse This
Formal (first contact)Dear Mr. / Ms. [Last name],
Professional (know each other)Hi [First name],
Very casual (close colleague)Hey [First name],
Group emailHi everyone, / Hi team,

"Dear Sir/Madam" sounds very old-fashioned in 2025. Use "Hi [Name]" for almost all professional emails unless you're writing to a very senior executive for the first time.

Opening Lines

✗ Sounds Foreign / Awkward

"I am writing to you because I want to ask about..." / "I hope this mail finds you well." / "As per the below email..." / "Please kindly advise."

✓ Sounds Natural

"I wanted to check in on..." / "Hope you're doing well!" (casual) / "I'm reaching out about..." / "Quick question about the project timeline."

"I hope this email finds you well" has become such a cliché that native speakers often find it mildly funny. You can skip the pleasantry entirely and get straight to the point — this is actually more professional, not less polite.

Writing the Body: Clear & Direct

The body should contain one main topic. If you have multiple unrelated topics, write separate emails. This is a cultural norm in English-speaking professional environments — it makes communication cleaner and easier to respond to.

The BLUF principle (Bottom Line Up Front): state your main point in the first sentence. Don't build up to it. Native English professional writing puts the key information first, then provides context and details after.

✗ Buries the main point

"Last week we discussed the project timeline during our meeting. As you know, there have been some delays related to the vendor. I wanted to mention that because of this, we may need to adjust the deadline. Would it be possible to extend it by two weeks?"

✓ Main point first

"I'd like to request a two-week extension on the project deadline. The vendor delays we discussed last week have pushed our timeline back, and we need the extra time to deliver quality work. Let me know if that works for you."

Keep paragraphs short — two to four sentences maximum. Long blocks of text are harder to read and less likely to get a response. Use bullet points for lists of three or more items.

Closings That Sound Native

How you close an email matters almost as much as how you open it. Non-native speakers often use closings that sound overly formal or stiff.

SituationUse This
Standard professionalBest, / Best regards, / Thanks,
Expecting a responseLooking forward to hearing from you,
After asking for somethingThanks in advance,
Very formalSincerely, / Kind regards,
Casual colleagueCheers, / Talk soon,
✗ Sounds overly formal / dated

"Yours faithfully," / "Respectfully yours," / "Thanking you in anticipation,"

For 90% of professional emails, "Best," or "Thanks," followed by your name is perfectly appropriate and sounds natural.

Common ESL Mistakes to Avoid

These patterns appear frequently in emails written by non-native speakers. Native readers notice them even if they don't say anything.

  • "Please kindly..." — redundant. Use either "please" or "kindly," not both. Better: just say what you need directly.
  • "As per my previous email..." — sounds passive-aggressive in English. Say: "As I mentioned," or just re-state the information briefly.
  • Excessive politeness — "I am so sorry to trouble you, but I was wondering if perhaps you might be able to..." This level of hedging reads as insecure. Be direct: "Could you please send me the file by Thursday?"
  • Translating idioms literally — expressions from your language often don't work in English. When in doubt, say it simply and plainly.
  • Missing articles (a/an/the) — one of the hardest things for speakers of article-free languages. "Please send report" should be "Please send the report."
  • Wrong prepositions — "discuss about," "reply to my mail," "revert back." The correct forms: "discuss," "reply to my email," "revert" (or just "respond").

3 Ready-to-Use Email Templates

Template 1: Following Up After No Response

Copy & Edit
Subject: Following up — [topic]

Hi [Name],

I wanted to follow up on my email from [date/last week] about [topic]. I know you're busy, so just checking in to make sure it didn't get lost.

Could you let me know your thoughts when you get a chance? Happy to hop on a quick call if that's easier.

Thanks,
[Your name]

Template 2: Requesting Something

Copy & Edit
Subject: Request — [what you need]

Hi [Name],

I'm working on [project/task] and need your help with [specific request].

[One sentence of context if needed.] Could you send that over by [deadline]? Let me know if you need anything from my end first.

Thanks in advance,
[Your name]

Template 3: Introducing Yourself (Cold Email)

Copy & Edit
Subject: [Specific reason for reaching out]

Hi [Name],

I'm [your name], [your role] at [company]. I came across your work on [where/how] and wanted to reach out.

[One or two sentences on why you're writing and what you're hoping for.]

Would you be open to a 20-minute call sometime this week or next? Happy to work around your schedule.

Best,
[Your name]
