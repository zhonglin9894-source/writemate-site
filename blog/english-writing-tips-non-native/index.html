8 English Writing Tips That Actually Work for Non-Native Speakers

By NativeProse Team · July 1, 2025 · 8 min read

Most English writing guides recycle the same advice: vary your vocabulary, avoid passive voice, check your grammar. That advice isn't wrong, but it mostly helps native speakers who already write naturally and just want to write better. For non-native speakers, the gap is different. The problem isn't quality — it's that the writing doesn't sound like it came from a native speaker at all.

These eight tips target that specific gap. Not grammar rules. The things that actually change how native readers experience your writing.

1
Read the genre you're trying to write

This sounds obvious, but almost no one does it systematically. If you write professional emails at work, spend 20 minutes reading professional email threads from native writers — real ones, from your colleagues or public examples online. If you write academic papers, read papers in your field. Not to copy sentences. To absorb the rhythm and pattern of how things get said in that specific context.

Every writing genre has its own conventions. Business English feels different from academic English, which feels different from journalistic English. Grammarly won't tell you when you're in the wrong register. Reading the genre trains your instincts for it.

2
Get to the point faster

In most East Asian, Middle Eastern, and Latin European communication cultures, it's polite to build context before making a point. You explain the situation, the background, and the reason — then arrive at what you need. English professional writing, especially American English, works in the opposite direction. Main point first. Context after. If you don't, native readers feel like they're being led somewhere without being told where they're going.

Background first

As you know, we have been working on the Q3 report for the past three weeks. During this process, we identified several areas that required additional input from the finance team. After discussing this internally, we concluded that we would need an extension of the deadline.

Point first

We need to push the Q3 report deadline by one week. We're waiting on finance team data that came in late — happy to walk through the details if helpful.

3
Stop over-hedging requests

Non-native speakers often add layers of softening language around requests — out of politeness, but also because asking directly feels blunt in their first language. In English, this extra hedging reads as uncertainty or weakness, not politeness.

Over-hedged

I was wondering if perhaps it might be possible for you to take a look at the attached document, if you have time and it's not too much trouble?

Direct and still polite

Could you take a look at the attached document when you get a chance?

"When you get a chance" does all the politeness work you need. The three-layer hedge before the request doesn't add politeness — it adds noise.

4
Use contractions in casual and professional contexts

Native English speakers use contractions constantly in professional writing. "I'm," "we're," "it's," "don't," "can't," "you'll." Avoiding contractions is a sign that someone is either in a very formal context (legal documents, official reports) or writing in their second language.

If you write "I am unable to attend the meeting" instead of "I can't make the meeting," native readers notice — not because it's wrong, but because no native speaker would write it that way in a casual context. Use contractions. They make your writing feel less formal and more human.

5
Cut every word that doesn't carry weight

ESL writers often write longer than necessary — partly from translating longer constructions from their first language, partly from wanting to be thorough and complete. English professional writing rewards brevity.

Phrases to delete on sight: "in order to" (use "to"), "at this point in time" (use "now"), "due to the fact that" (use "because"), "it should be noted that" (just say the thing), "for the purpose of" (use "to" or "for").

Every sentence you read back, ask: does each word pull its weight? If not, cut it.

6
Vary your sentence length

One pattern that marks non-native writing is uniform sentence length — every sentence roughly the same length, same structure. Native writers vary dramatically. Short sentences for emphasis. Then longer ones that build a point gradually, add context, and bring the reader through a more complex idea before landing somewhere clear.

Look at that last paragraph. Short sentence. Then a long one. That rhythm is something native readers are accustomed to even when they don't consciously notice it. If your paragraphs look like a series of evenly spaced fence posts, vary the length.

7
Replace translated idioms with simple English

Every language has expressions that feel natural internally but land strangely when translated into English. When in doubt, say the thing plainly rather than reaching for an idiom from your first language.

Some examples: "We need to eat bitterness on this project" (Chinese: 吃苦) → "This project is going to be hard work." "I will give you a call back" → "I'll call you back." "Please make sure to revert" → "Please reply" or "Let me know." Plain language is always safe. Translated idioms are always a risk.

8
Get real-time feedback on naturalness — not just grammar

Grammar checkers tell you when a sentence breaks a rule. They don't tell you when a sentence is technically correct but still sounds foreign. The fastest way to get that second kind of feedback is to run your text through a tool that rewrites for naturalness, and compare the output against what you wrote.

This isn't about replacing your writing — it's about training your instincts. When you see the same type of correction appear repeatedly (your hedged requests getting simplified, your formal register getting warmed up, your translated idioms getting replaced), you start to internalize the pattern and catch it yourself next time.
