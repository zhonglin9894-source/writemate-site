Why Your English Still Sounds Foreign (Even After Years of Study)

By NativeProse Team · July 1, 2025 · 9 min read

You've probably Googled some version of this question before. "Why does my English sound unnatural." "Why do native speakers write so differently." "How do I stop sounding like I translated from another language." The fact that you're asking means you're past the beginner stage. Grammar? Mostly sorted. Vocabulary? There. But something's still slightly off — and you can feel it even when you can't name it.

The frustrating truth is that English fluency and English naturalness are two completely different things. Schools teach fluency. No one really teaches naturalness.

Grammar Is Not the Problem

Most writing guides for ESL learners focus on grammar — tenses, subject-verb agreement, articles, prepositions. These matter. But here's the thing: a sentence can be grammatically perfect and still sound immediately foreign to a native reader.

Compare these two sentences. Both are correct. Only one sounds like something a native speaker would actually write:

Sounds foreign
I am writing to inform you that I have completed the task you assigned to me last Tuesday.
Sounds native
Just wanted to let you know — finished the task from last Tuesday.

The first sentence has zero grammar errors. The second is how a native speaker would actually write that email. Same information. Completely different feel.

What's different? Register, rhythm, and word choice — three things that grammar rules don't cover at all.

The Three Real Culprits

1. Register Mismatch

Every language has different levels of formality — casual, professional, formal, ceremonial. The tricky part is that these levels don't map cleanly between languages. What counts as politely professional in Japanese or Chinese can land as stiff and robotic in English. Native English speakers in professional settings use a tone that's closer to "warm casual" than "formal." If you're not calibrated to that, your emails feel like they're from a legal document even when you're just asking about a meeting time.

This is why phrases like "I hope this email finds you well" or "Please do the needful" feel dated or foreign — they come from a formal register that native business writers mostly abandoned in the 2000s.

2. Calques — Translated Thinking

A calque is when you translate an expression from your native language word-for-word into English. The grammar works out, but the result is something no native speaker would ever say naturally. Every language has them, and they're nearly invisible to the writer because the expression makes perfect sense in the original language.

Some common examples from different language backgrounds:

  • "Make a meeting" (from Chinese: 开会) → should be "have a meeting" or "schedule a call"
  • "I will give you a call back" (direct translation) → "I'll call you back"
  • "Please give advice" → "Let me know what you think" or "Any feedback?"
  • "According to my understanding" → "As far as I know" or "From what I understand"

None of these are wrong, exactly. But they reveal the translated origin in a way that native phrases don't.

3. Over-Hedging

Non-native speakers often add layers of politeness that native speakers read as uncertainty or excessive formality. It comes from a good place — you want to be respectful — but in English, direct communication is considered more professional, not less polite.

Over-hedged
I was wondering if perhaps it would be possible for you to consider reviewing the document when you have a free moment, if it's not too much trouble.
Direct and natural
Could you review the document when you get a chance? No rush.

The second version is actually more polite because it respects the reader's time by being clear and concise. "No rush" does more work than a paragraph of hedging.

Why Years of Study Don't Fix This

Language classes teach rules. Naturalness comes from exposure — specifically, from absorbing thousands of hours of native-speaker output until the patterns become instinctive. Living in an English-speaking country for a few years will do it. Watching enough English TV and reading enough native content will slowly do it too.

But that's a slow process, measured in years. And for most people who need professional English right now — for work emails, LinkedIn messages, job applications, Slack threads — years isn't an option.

Grammar checkers like Grammarly don't help with this either. They check rules. They don't know whether your phrasing sounds like something a native speaker would actually say. "I am hoping to receive your feedback at your earliest convenience" passes Grammarly fine. It still sounds foreign.

What Actually Works

The fastest way to close this gap is to directly compare your writing against native-speaker alternatives — not rules, but actual phrasing choices a native writer would make for your specific sentence.

Read native content in your target register. If you write professional emails, read professional emails. If you write academic papers, read published papers in your field. Not to copy sentences, but to absorb the pattern of how things are said.
