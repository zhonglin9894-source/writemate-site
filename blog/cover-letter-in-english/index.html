How to Write a Cover Letter in English (Non-Native Speaker Guide)

By NativeProse Team · July 1, 2025 · 10 min read

Cover letters are the hardest English writing challenge for non-native speakers. They're short — usually one page — high stakes, and deeply personal. They're supposed to sound like you. Specifically: like a confident, articulate version of you writing in your second or third language.

The problem isn't grammar. Most non-native applicants have clean grammar. The problem is register and naturalness — the slight formality mismatch, the phrasing that works in one language and lands awkwardly in English, the sentences that are technically correct but feel like they were written by a committee. Recruiters notice. They read hundreds of these. Anything that feels off gets filed away as "not quite."

This guide goes through the structure, the common mistakes, and a template you can actually use.

What Recruiters Actually Read (and What They Skip)

First, a reality check: most cover letters get about 30 seconds. Recruiters scan. They're looking for three things: does this person understand what the role is, do they have evidence they can do it, and does this feel like someone I'd want to talk to. That's it.

This means long, formal cover letters — the kind that open with "I am writing to express my sincere interest in the position of..." — already lose on point three before the recruiter finishes the first sentence. The goal isn't to demonstrate your vocabulary. It's to sound like a real person who genuinely wants this specific job.

The Structure That Works

Three paragraphs. Maybe four if you have something specific to add. That's all you need.

  • Opening (2–3 sentences): Why this role at this company — not a generic statement, something specific. Shows you actually read the job posting.
  • Middle (2–3 sentences): The one or two most relevant things you've done that directly address what they're hiring for. Specific, not general.
  • Closing (1–2 sentences): Forward-looking. What you'd bring, and a simple ask for a conversation.

That's it. Anything longer is usually just anxiety — the instinct to fill space with credentials because you're not sure the core is strong enough. Resist it.

Opening: Don't Start With "I"

The most common cover letter opener across all writers — native and non-native — is "I am writing to apply for..." This is the fastest way to signal that your letter is generic. Every recruiter has read this sentence ten thousand times.

Generic opener

I am writing to express my sincere interest in the Senior Product Manager position at Acme Corp, as advertised on LinkedIn. I believe my extensive experience and skills make me a strong candidate for this role.

Specific opener

Acme's pivot to enterprise last year is exactly the kind of market shift I've been navigating at my current company — and it's why this PM role caught my attention. I've spent the past three years building 0-to-1 products in B2B SaaS, and I'd love to bring that to your team.

The second opener shows you know something specific about the company, connects it to your experience, and sounds like a human being. It also gets the key information (B2B SaaS, 0-to-1 products) in before the recruiter loses interest.

Middle: One Specific Thing, Not a List

Non-native writers often treat the middle of the cover letter like a resume summary — listing multiple accomplishments and skills. This weakens it. The middle of a cover letter works best when it focuses on one or two specific examples that directly answer "why should we hire you for this role."

Too broad

I have experience in project management, team leadership, stakeholder communication, and cross-functional collaboration. I am skilled in Agile methodologies and have worked on various successful projects throughout my career.

Specific and relevant

At my last company, I led the migration of our core infrastructure from on-prem to AWS — a project that had been stalled for two years before I took it on. We shipped in five months and cut infrastructure costs by 40%. That kind of messy, cross-team technical project is what I enjoy most, and I see a lot of it in this role.

The specific version gives the recruiter something to remember and something to ask about in an interview. The generic version gives them nothing to hold.

Common Phrases to Avoid

These appear constantly in cover letters from non-native speakers — and from many native speakers too. They add words without adding meaning:

  • "I am a quick learner" — everyone says this; prove it with an example instead
  • "I am passionate about [industry]" — fine to feel, awkward to announce
  • "I would be honored to join your esteemed organization" — too formal for almost any company in 2025
  • "Please find attached my resume/CV" — just "I've attached my CV" or say nothing
  • "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience" — use "I'd love to chat further if you think it's a fit"

A Template You Can Actually Use

Cover Letter Template
Hi [Name / Hiring Manager],

[One specific thing about the company or role that made you apply — not "I saw your posting on LinkedIn"]. [Connect it to your background in one sentence].

In my last role at [Company], I [specific accomplishment with a result — ideally a number]. [One sentence connecting that experience to what this role needs].

I'd love to talk about what I could bring to the team. Happy to share more context on any of this.

[Your name]

That's the whole letter. Under 150 words. It's short on purpose — short letters that are specific beat long letters that are generic, every time.

A Note on Tone

The tone that works for cover letters in English-speaking companies (US, UK, Australia, Canada) is warm and direct. Not stiff. Not overly humble. Not "I would be so grateful for any opportunity you might consider offering." Confident, but not arrogant. Think: the way you'd introduce yourself at the start of a good interview.

If you're applying to more formal industries — law, banking, certain government roles — the tone shifts slightly more formal. But even there, "Dear Ms. Chen, I'm writing to apply for the associate position" is perfectly appropriate. You don't need three sentences of preamble before the actual content.
