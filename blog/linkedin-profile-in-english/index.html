Writing Your LinkedIn Profile in English as a Non-Native Speaker

By NativeProse Team · July 1, 2025 · 9 min read

There's a specific kind of LinkedIn profile that international professionals write. You can spot it in seconds. The summary opens with "I am a highly motivated professional with extensive experience in..." The experience section says "Participated in organizing cross-functional initiatives" instead of "Led cross-functional projects." The skills section lists "Result-oriented" and "Team player" and "Hardworking."

These profiles aren't bad. They're just visibly non-native — and in a platform where first impressions happen in three seconds, that matters more than it should.

This guide goes section by section through what makes LinkedIn profiles sound foreign, and how to fix each one.

The Headline

LinkedIn's default fills your headline with your job title and company. Most native professionals override this with something more specific. The goal is to say what you do and who you help — not just your role title.

Too generic

Software Engineer at ABC Company

More specific and searchable

Backend Engineer | Python & AWS | Building scalable systems for fintech

The pipe-separated format is a LinkedIn convention that native users adopted organically. It signals you know how the platform works. Use keywords your target audience would search for — recruiter, not just job titles.

The About / Summary Section

This is where most non-native profiles lose readers. The common pattern is: open with "I am a..." followed by a list of traits and then a paragraph about career history. Native English writers on LinkedIn tend to do something different — they open with a specific observation, a result, or a question that hooks the reader in the first two lines (because that's all that's visible before "see more").

Sounds translated

I am a highly motivated marketing professional with 7 years of extensive experience in digital marketing and brand management. I have worked in various industries and have a strong passion for results-driven strategies and teamwork.

Sounds natural

I've spent the last 7 years helping B2B companies turn their content into actual pipeline — not just traffic.

Most of my work sits at the intersection of SEO, demand gen, and product marketing. I like building systems that keep working after the campaign ends.

Notice the shift: from traits ("motivated," "passionate") to results and specifics ("B2B companies," "pipeline," "not just traffic"). Traits are cheap — everyone claims them. Specifics are credible.

Also: write in first person but drop the "I" at the start of sentences sometimes. "Spent the last 7 years..." instead of "I have spent the last 7 years..." feels more direct. It's a small thing that native LinkedIn writers do naturally.

Experience Descriptions

This is the section with the most consistent mistake: passive and weak verbs. "Participated in," "involved in," "responsible for," "assisted with" — these are experience description killers. They bury your actual contribution under hedging language.

The fix is simple in theory: start every bullet with a strong action verb, then the result, then the context.

Passive and weak

• Participated in the development of a new customer onboarding process
• Was responsible for managing client relationships
• Involved in cross-team communication and coordination efforts

Active and specific

• Redesigned the customer onboarding process, cutting time-to-activation from 14 days to 6
• Managed 40+ enterprise client accounts; maintained 94% retention rate over 2 years
• Coordinated between product, sales, and customer success to launch 3 new features on schedule

Add numbers wherever you can. Not because recruiters count them (though they do), but because specificity forces you to think about what you actually did — and that specificity is what makes a bullet land.

Strong verb list to swap in: led, built, launched, reduced, increased, managed, designed, negotiated, shipped, scaled, closed, drove, cut, created, grew. One of these at the start of every bullet.

Skills Section

"Hardworking." "Team player." "Result-oriented." "Dedicated." These skills appear on thousands of LinkedIn profiles and mean essentially nothing to recruiters. They're the LinkedIn equivalent of "I am a fast learner" on a CV — so expected that they become invisible.

What actually helps in the Skills section: specific technical skills, tools, platforms, methodologies. Things that can be verified and searched. "Google Analytics," "Figma," "Mandarin (Native)," "SQL," "Agile/Scrum" — these are useful. "Good communicator" is not.

If your industry uses specific certifications or frameworks, list them by name. Recruiters search for these exact strings.

Connection Requests & Messages

LinkedIn messages from non-native speakers often follow a formal pattern that reads as stilted in the platform's casual professional context:

Too formal for LinkedIn

Dear Mr. Johnson, I am writing to you to express my keen interest in connecting with you on this professional networking platform. I have reviewed your profile and I believe we share many common professional interests. I would be honored if you would accept my connection request.

Natural LinkedIn tone

Hi David — came across your work on the Series B announcement at Acme. I lead product at a similar-stage startup and would love to connect. Always good to know folks in the same space.

LinkedIn is professional but informal. "Hi [first name]" is standard. Short messages get read. Specific context (how you found them, why you're reaching out) gets responses. The five-sentence formal letter approach gets ignored.
